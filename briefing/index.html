  3. Which jobs will AI actually touch?

    The impact of AI depends on your specific job category. Some roles show nearly total exposure, while others show very little. This evidence is mixed and based on preliminary studies.

    Supported. The evidence shows that AI impacts vary widely by occupation and may lead to complex human-AI collaborations rather than total automation.

  4. Does your screen create a distorted mirror?

    The evidence shows that online dating is linked to negative impacts on body image and mental health.

    Established. The evidence shows that online dating has specific psychological risks and that relationship outcomes are influenced by communication patterns rather than being simply similar to offline dating.

  5. Does your screen actually help you remember?

    Established. The evidence generally shows that reading on paper leads to slightly better comprehension than reading on a screen, though this effect varies significantly depending on the text genre, the reader's age, and the specific design of the digital medium.

  6. Who sets the price you are willing to pay?

    Your brain uses a preceding number as a reference point to make judgments about what you are willing to pay.

    Established. The evidence confirms that anchoring influences judgments and willingness to pay, though the effect size varies based on product type and measurement methodology.

  8. What is the cost of the extra hour?

    Established. The evidence consistently links long working hours to increased risks of stroke, atrial fibrillation, and broader occupational health issues.

  9. Does a like feel like a reward?

    Established. The evidence shows that 'likes' and popular content trigger reward-related brain activity and influence behavior, particularly in adolescents.

  10. Does your phone drain your brain when you are not using it?

  12. Is there a limit to how long you can live?

    Supported. The evidence suggests that human lifespan is not currently capped at 122 and may be extendable through biological and pharmacological interventions.

  13. Why do we choose people who are different?

    In existing relationships, how similar you think your partner is predicts attraction more than how similar you actually are.

    Established. The evidence shows that while assortative mating leads to similarities in traits like education and height, the psychological attraction in long-term relationships depends more on how similar people perceive each other to be.

  14. Why do you forgive people but punish machines?

    Supported. The evidence shows that while humans are more forgiving of errors than algorithms, the impact of showing those errors depends heavily on how the information is presented and the nature of the task.

    Established. The evidence shows that training produces 'near-transfer' improvements in the specific skills being practiced, but 'far-transfer' to general cognitive abilities is consistently small or non-existent.

  22. Who is setting your internal pace?

  25. Is dopamine about pleasure?

    Supported. The evidence shows that dopamine is a mechanism for calculating reward prediction errors and encoding subjective value to facilitate learning and reward-seeking behavior.

  30. How much nature do you actually need?
