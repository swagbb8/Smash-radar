  1. Is your memory lying to you?

    The clarity of a memory does not correlate with its factual consistency. Your most intense recollections are just as prone to decay as your mundane ones.

    Supported. The evidence shows that while flashbulb memories feel more accurate and vivid than everyday memories, they do not actually maintain higher levels of consistency over time.

  2. Who is more persuasive: you or a machine?

    In some cases, the persuasive impact of gpt-4 rivaled and even surpassed that of human experts.

    Established. The evidence shows that LLMs possess significant persuasive capabilities that can match or beat humans in specific contexts, but they are not yet more effective at large-scale distribution than traditional methods.

  3. Does social rejection feel like a physical wound?

    While social pain and physical pain share psychological consequences, research fails to confirm they use the same primary pain matrix.

    Established. The evidence shows that social and physical pain share psychological outcomes and some brain regions, but significant research fails to confirm they use the same primary pain matrix.

  4. Why does your brain prioritize others?

    Activity in the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex and basal forebrain drives learning specifically in prosocial contexts.

    Established. The evidence identifies specific neural circuits and evolutionary mechanisms for prosociality but shows that these behaviors are not uniform and are influenced by external factors like socioeconomic status.

  5. Does shared trauma actually create a bond?

    Postpartum depression at 2 months after delivery is a more important factor affecting bonding failure than posttraumatic stress symptoms after childbirth. The evidence is mixed and shows associations rather than direct causation.

    Established. The sources indicate that bonding impairment is more consistently associated with maternal depression and specific types of childhood neglect than with the direct experience of trauma.

  6. Does your best friend affect your romance?

    Research shows you feel jealousy specifically when a third party threatens your position in a friendship. This behavior is a tool to counter that threat.

    Supported. The sources confirm that friendship jealousy exists as a response to being replaced by a third party, but they do not provide direct evidence that a 'best friend' specifically decreases romantic relationship quality.

  7. Does sugar really make children hyperactive?

    While sugar does not affect behavior or cognitive performance in children, a combination of ultra-processed foods consistently shows the strongest negative effects across most cognitive domains.

    Established. The evidence confirms that sugar does not affect children's behavior or cognition, though other ultra-processed foods may have negative cognitive effects.

  8. Who funded your heart health advice?

    During the 1960s and 1970s, industry-sponsored programs successfully promoted fat as the primary cause of coronary heart disease while downplaying the risks of sugar.

    Established. The evidence shows that the sugar industry actively funded and influenced research to downplay the risks of sucrose and shift the blame for heart disease toward fat.

  9. Are you sober when you are tired?

    After 20-25 hours of wakefulness, your performance decrements can equal a blood alcohol concentration of 0.10%.

    Supported. The evidence shows that sleep deprivation causes significant cognitive and motor impairments that can match alcohol intoxication, but the severity of these effects is task-dependent and may be overestimated in some studies.

  10. What happens when a species disappears forever?

    Extrapolation from a random sample of land snail species suggests 130,000 extinctions have already occurred.

    Supported. The evidence indicates that vertebrate species and genera are being lost at rates far exceeding natural background levels, though the total number of known extinctions remains low.

  11. Why do the poorest people play the most?

    People with the least money play the most. They pay a regressive tax on games that cost them more relative to their income.

    Supported. The evidence consistently shows that lower-income individuals participate more in lotteries and face a regressive tax burden, though higher-priced games may be less regressive.

  12. Does your child know why they want that snack?

    Understanding the tactics of marketing fails to shield children from its impact on their brand and product attitudes.

    Established. The evidence consistently shows that exposure to unhealthy food marketing leads to increased dietary intake and influences preferences in children.

  13. Where do you go when your phone dies?

    Heavy GPS use is linked to a steeper decline in hippocampal-dependent spatial memory and reduced brain connectivity.

    Supported. The evidence shows a correlation between high GPS usage and poorer spatial memory and reduced hippocampal connectivity, but it does not prove that GPS use causes a loss of navigation skills.

  14. Why does a lie reach you before the truth?

    False news spreads more broadly and deeper than the truth in every category of information. You are moving through a landscape where lies travel with more speed than facts.

    Established. The evidence confirms that false news spreads more effectively than truth due to human behavior and novelty, though people are generally capable of discerning falsehoods.

  15. What did you miss in the last hour?

    People often fail to notice significant changes in visual scenes, even when those changes happen right in front of their eyes.

    Established. The evidence confirms that people frequently fail to notice significant changes in scenes, though these failures can be mitigated by increased attention or saliency.

  16. Are you actually learning the material?

    Students who used GPT-4 perform worse than those who never had access. This result is mixed and depends on how the tool is used.

    Supported. The evidence shows that AI can improve immediate performance and proficiency, but without proper guardrails or pedagogical design, it can hinder the actual acquisition of skills.

  17. Will you stay still or step in?

    Bystanders were 19 times more likely to intervene in real-life conflicts when danger was present than when it was not. The evidence is mixed.

    Established. The evidence shows that while the 'bystander effect' exists, it is significantly mitigated by the presence of danger and specific situational factors.

  18. Is a classic psychiatry study a lie?

    Supported. The evidence is conflicted, with one source explicitly labeling the study as fraud while another study disputes the conclusions of a modern critic who used Rosenhan's work as a basis.

  19. Is your screen actually hurting your mind?

    Most reviews interpret the associations between social media use and mental health as weak or inconsistent.

    Established. The evidence shows a complex picture where some specific types of social media use are linked to mental health problems, but the overall evidence is often weak or inconclusive.

  20. Who will you call tonight?

    Research shows the risk of mortality from poor social relationships is similar to the risk from light smoking. It also exceeds the risks from hypertension and obesity.

    Established. The evidence consistently shows that poor social relationships and isolation are significant predictors of mortality across various demographics and health conditions.

  21. Is your confidence a result of your environment?

    Established. The evidence shows that self-enhancement is a common human phenomenon but is not universal, as it is notably absent in East Asian cultures.

  22. Does giving money actually cure depression?

    While 85% of interventions showed some positive impact on mental health outcomes in children, the evidence shows no significant impact on depressive symptoms specifically.

    Established. The evidence shows that financial strain and debt are linked to mental health problems, but the impact of financial interventions on specific symptoms like depression can be inconsistent.

  23. Does stress always change your brain?

    While some studies show reduced hippocampal volume, others show no effect or even volume increases in resilient subjects.

    Supported. The evidence is mixed, showing that chronic stress can reduce hippocampal volume in some contexts but does not consistently cause it across all subjects or conditions.

  24. Why do you forget what you just searched?

    Established. The evidence confirms that internet use impacts memory mechanisms, but it also shows that individual knowledge levels and specific behaviors moderate these effects.

  25. Does the city sound hurt your heart?

    Established. The evidence shows a positive association between road traffic noise and cardiovascular risks like hypertension and incident CVD, though the strength and significance of these links vary across different studies.

  26. How much of the ocean floor have you actually seen?

    Established. The evidence confirms that direct visual observation of the deep seafloor is extremely limited and heavily skewed toward specific nations.

  27. Does more money actually make you happier?

    Supported. The evidence is conflicting, with some studies showing a linear increase in well-being for all earners and others identifying a specific plateau for emotional well-being at $75,000.

  28. Is the air in your room affecting your choices?

    While some studies show that higher carbon dioxide levels reduce decision-making performance, others show performance can remain similar or even exceed baseline at higher concentrations.

    Supported. The evidence shows that while elevated CO2 is linked to and can cause decrements in decision-making, the effects are inconsistent and may vary depending on the specific concentration and subject population.
