The briefing

The newest files, each in two sentences.

  1. Does scrolling actually change your mental health?

    Most results show an effect size of less than.10. The evidence is mixed and often contested.

    Established. The evidence is mixed and often negligible, showing that the impact of social media use depends heavily on the specific context and the type of content rather than just the mode of engagement.

  2. Who is actually changing your mind?

    Evidence confirms that algorithms can facilitate persuasion. However, there is no evidence that models can predict your conversions before you are conscious of them.

    Supported. The sources confirm that algorithms can mediate persuasion and that information overload can increase its effectiveness, but they do not specifically prove that models can predict conversions before they are conscious.

  3. Where does your internal map go?

    Using GPS navigation in AR glasses for three months showed a decrease in functional coupling between the hippocampus and associated brain regions. The evidence on this is emerging.

    Supported. The evidence shows that technology-assisted navigation is associated with measurable changes in brain connectivity but does not currently show a measurable degradation in spatial navigation skills.

  4. What is currently inside your brain cells?

    Higher concentrations are observed in cerebrovascular walls and immune cells. Evidence regarding the pathways of exposure and clearance is still limited.

    Established. The sources confirm that microplastics and nanoplastics are present in human tissues and can cause cellular damage, but they do not consistently show biomagnification in marine food webs.

  5. Is your outrage a survival instinct?

    The evidence does not support the idea that online outrage is a biological survival threat linked to the amygdala. Instead, it involves complex neural patterns in the prefrontal cortex.

    Supported. The evidence confirms that the brain uses specific prefrontal oscillations and visceral interoception to process conflict and moral appraisal, but it does not specifically link online outrage to amygdala-mediated survival threats.

  6. Does being a good listener drain you?

    In certain professions, this emotional labor is linked to emotional exhaustion and burnout. This finding is specific to early childhood teachers and may not apply to everyone.

    Supported. The sources confirm that active-empathic listening involves significant emotional involvement and that emotional labor is linked to exhaustion, but they do not explicitly prove that being a 'good listener' causes stress for everyone.

  7. Is what you see actually real?

    The cerebral cortex acts as a generative model that predicts sensory data to create your perception of the world.

    Established. The sources provide a theoretical framework for how the brain uses prediction and error signaling to process sensory input like vision, music, and rhythm.

  8. Does your brain prioritize your energy?

    The metabolic cost of complex bird song is no greater than simpler songs. The evidence on whether personality is tied to metabolic efficiency is mixed or contested.

    Supported. The evidence shows that while some physical movements optimize for energy, complex communicative behaviors do not always show a corresponding increase in metabolic cost.

  9. Is your conscience a physical sensation?

    While bodily sensations like disgust influence the intensity of your moral decisions, they are not the sole or primary drivers of your judgment. The evidence on this is mixed.

    Established. The evidence shows that while bodily sensations like disgust influence the intensity of moral judgments, they are not the sole or primary driver of moral decision-making.

  10. Does your software make you more capable?

    While systems are often used for control, specific designs like support-after-processing can improve worker satisfaction and confidence.

    Established. The evidence shows a complex duality where algorithmic management often functions as a tool of control, yet its specific design can either diminish or enhance worker autonomy and self-efficacy.

  11. Who chooses what you buy?

    Consumer behavior is driven by a balance between impulsive rewards and executive functions. This is a contested finding regarding the exact timing of these choices.

    Supported. The evidence shows that consumer behavior is driven by neurobiological and psychological factors like cognitive biases and reward-seeking systems, but it does not specifically confirm that the brain selects options seconds before conscious awareness.

  12. Is your love actually a form of addiction?

    In early stages, intense love shows symptoms like euphoria, craving, tolerance, and withdrawal. Researchers have not officially categorized it as a chemical or behavioral addiction.

    Established. The evidence shows that romantic love shares many neurobiological mechanisms and symptoms with addiction, but it also possesses unique social and evolutionary components.

  13. Who is the city actually built for?

    Spatial entities like halal zones are integrated to ensure strict product traceability and adherence to principles.

    Supported. The sources show that urban forms are shaped by a mix of historical origins, administrative needs, and logistical requirements for goods, but they do not explicitly confirm a 19th-century design for surveillance.

  14. Does scrolling actually satisfy your hunger?

    Research shows no measurable impact on the biological mechanisms that signal satiety.

    Supported. The evidence shows that social media is associated with negative mental health outcomes and body image issues, but it does not demonstrate a measurable impact on satiety mechanisms.

  15. Who are you actually listening to?

    Micro-influencers only change your behavior when they drive active brand engagement. Trust alone does not guarantee a purchase.

    Supported. The evidence confirms that micro-influencers are more effective than macro-influencers at driving consumer behavior due to perceived authenticity and interaction power.

  16. Why do you feel lonely in a crowd?

    Exposure to neoliberal ideology increases feelings of competition, which correlates with higher loneliness and lower well-being. This evidence is mixed and may only apply to liberals.

    Established. The evidence shows that perceived competition and relative deprivation are significant drivers of loneliness, but it does not rule out the impact of objective social isolation.

  17. Is your metabolism inherited from your ancestors' hunger?

    Non-genetic heredity from events like famine is often mediated by epigenetic regulations of gene expression. These changes may be transferred even across several generations.

    Supported. The sources provide evidence that famine and early-life nutrition can cause epigenetic changes and metabolic programming that may be passed across generations.

  18. Is your lack of focus a character flaw?

    High cortisol levels in children are associated with reduced ability to manage cognitive tasks.

    Established. The sources confirm that poverty and scarcity are linked to reduced cognitive capacity and impaired decision-making, but they do not provide direct neuroimaging evidence of a specific brain change.

  19. Is your taste being curated or created?

    While filtering often limits diversity, systems can be built to increase variety and support your creative growth.

    Supported. The evidence shows that while algorithms can narrow information exposure, they are also capable of being designed to increase diversity and support user self-cultivation.

  20. Does offloading actually hurt your memory?

    This occurs when you have an explicit goal to retain the information and are aware of a future test.

    Supported. The evidence shows that offloading is a complex trade-off where it improves immediate task performance but can harm memory unless the user has an explicit goal to retain the information.

  21. Why does bad news feel like a necessity?

    Research shows a consistent association between habitual media use and poorer mental health outcomes. However, these studies are observational and do not prove that doomscrolling causes these issues.

    Established. The evidence shows a consistent association between doomscrolling and negative mental health outcomes, but it does not establish a causal link.

  22. Are you actually working while you are at your desk?

    While presenteeism is linked to productivity loss through anxiety states or increased headache severity, it does not always have a direct relationship with health-related loss of productivity.

    Established. The evidence shows that presenteeism is a complex phenomenon where the link to lost productivity often depends on specific health conditions and psychological states.

  23. Does your workout actually cause inflammation?

    When you push your body too far, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species may contribute to overtraining syndrome in skeletal muscle.

    Established. The evidence shows that exercise typically reduces inflammatory markers and oxidative stress rather than increasing them, though overtraining syndrome is linked to inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms.

  24. What actually predicts your behavior?

    The research focuses on commitment readiness, affective reactions, and stability factors rather than comparing shared values to shared lifestyles. The evidence on this specific comparison is mixed or contested.

    Established. The provided sources do not contain any data comparing shared values to shared lifestyles as predictors of relationship stability.

  25. Can your brain actually do two things at once?

    The evidence shows that switching between different cognitive strategies or tasks causes measurable delays in your response times and alters your neural connectivity.

    Established. The evidence confirms that task-switching involves a measurable cognitive cost in terms of time and neural connectivity, rather than seamless multitasking.

  26. Is your willpower being eroded by your environment?

    Research indicates that high stress may impair self-control by specifically reducing the use of cognitive control for early desire regulation strategies. This effect may leave late resistance strategies unaffected.

    Established. The evidence shows that chronic stress is associated with deficits in executive function and memory, but it does not explicitly prove that 'scarcity' itself is the primary driver of these changes.

  27. Is your brain's idle mode a precursor to depression?

    While these brain patterns are linked to rumination, the changes are highly variable and inconsistent across different people.

    Established. The evidence shows that DMN alterations are associated with depression and rumination, but the effects are inconsistent and do not provide a simple, uniform biomarker.

  28. Does your watch know your anxiety better than you do?

    This is a specific anxiety framework driven by the continuous stream of data from wearable devices. It differs from seeking health information online. This finding is currently theoretical and awaits future empirical validation.

    Established. The evidence shows that wearables can be used for positive health monitoring and anxiety detection, but they also create a new framework for anxiety driven by constant data access.

  29. Does multitasking actually lower your IQ?

    The evidence on whether these habits consistently reduce functional IQ is mixed and inconsistent.

    Supported. The sources show that multitasking and task-switching increase prefrontal cortex workload and can lead to more errors, but they do not provide a definitive measure of a functional IQ drop.

  30. Whose clock are you living by?

    Technologies that pull against your internal sense of time can reshape your behavior.

    Supported. The evidence shows that modern environmental factors and externalized time technologies can disrupt circadian rhythms and reshape behavior, but it lacks specific data on the 19th-century industrial transition.

  31. Is your politeness creating a barrier to real connection?

    Research shows that using too many emojis when meeting someone new can reduce the quality of the interaction.

    Established. The evidence suggests that social norms and digital mediation influence the depth and quality of intimacy, but it does not definitively prove that politeness itself inhibits authentic connection.

  32. Does knowing the truth make you believe it less?

    Research shows no identified circumstances where fear leads to undesirable outcomes.

    Established. The evidence suggests that providing facts or using fear appeals generally leads to positive outcomes rather than reinforcing original misconceptions.

  33. Does talking to your tribe make you more biased?

    Evidence shows that sharing opinions in structured online networks can improve accuracy and decrease bias among people with similar views.

    Supported. The evidence contradicts the proposed angle by showing that social interaction in networks can improve collective intelligence and reduce bias, even if platform algorithms and specific content types contribute to polarization.

  34. Does your career actually age your cells?

    Research shows no significant differences in telomere length across occupational categories or stress levels.

    Supported. The evidence fails to support the idea that high-powered leadership or job strain causes rapid biological aging, as studies found no significant differences in telomere length across various occupational categories or stress levels.
