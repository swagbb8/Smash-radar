The uncomfortable truth

Does doing good make it easier to do wrong?

File no. 0317November 22, 2026Modern society

Good deeds can license your next selfish act.

When you do something good, you may feel entitled to act poorly later. This effect is stronger when people are watching you. The evidence for this is mixed and some studies have failed to replicate it.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Three attempts to replicate the moral licensing effect did not confirm the original results.

    “The first two replication attempts (student samples, 95% power based on the initial findings, NStudy1= 105, NStudy2= 150), did not confirm the original results. The third replication attempt (MTurk sample, 95% power based on a meta-analysis on self-licensing, N = 940) also did not confirm the moral licensing effect.”

    Meta-analysis, 2014. Blanken et al., Social Psychology.

  2. When supervisors demonstrated prior ethical behaviors, subordinates liberated them to act in abusive ways.

    “The results showed that when supervisors demonstrated prior ethical behaviors, subordinates, as victims, liberated them to act in abusive ways.”

    Experiment, 2019. Wang & Chan, Frontiers in Psychology.

  3. Moral licensing is stronger when participants are observed (g = 0.65) than unobserved (g = 0.13).

    “In a multi-level meta-analysis of 115 experiments ( N = 21,770), moral licensing was stronger when participants were observed ( g = 0.65) than unobserved ( g = 0.13).”

    Meta-analysis, 2026. Rotella et al., Personality & social psychology bulletin.

  4. Individuals are more likely to exhibit licensing when they think concretely about what they have accomplished with their initial behavior.

    “whereas they are more likely to exhibit licensing when they think concretely about what they have accomplished with their initial behavior-as long as the second behavior does not blatantly threaten a cherished identity.”

    Review, 2015. Mullen & Monin, Annual Review of Psychology.

  5. A meta-analysis of 91 studies found that the magnitude of the moral licensing effect is estimated to be a Cohen's d of 0.31.

    “Based on this analysis, the magnitude of the moral licensing effect is estimated to be a Cohen's d of 0.31.”

    Meta-analysis, 2015. Blanken et al., Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.

The psychology of moral licensing.

Doing something ethical can create a sense of credit. You then use that credit to justify less ethical behavior. This happens most clearly when you think about your specific accomplishments.

Your goodness is not a permanent state.

That may be why you feel less guilty after a period of being helpful. Your brain might view morality as a balance sheet rather than a fixed character trait.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The office environment.

You spend the morning mentoring a new coworker. In the afternoon, you ignore a colleague's request for help because you feel you have already done enough for the day.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that while moral licensing exists and is influenced by social observation and concrete thinking, its effects are inconsistent across different studies and replication attempts.

Many studies lacked baseline conditions, making it ambiguous whether licensing was actually observed.
