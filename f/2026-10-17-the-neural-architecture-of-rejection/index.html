Does social rejection feel like a physical wound?

File no. 0112October 17, 2026The human brain

Social rejection may not use the same pain matrix.

While social pain and physical pain share psychological consequences, research fails to confirm they use the same primary pain matrix.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. A statistical analysis of 244 participants fails to support the claim that social rejection operates on the same pain matrix as nociceptive stimuli.

    “Our statistical multi-level kernel density analysis (MKDA) of Cyberball neuroimaging studies with 244 participants fails to support the claim that social rejection operates on the same pain matrix as nociceptive stimuli”

    Meta-analysis, 2013. Cacioppo et al., Scientific Reports.

  2. Reliving an unwanted rejection by a romantic partner was significantly characterized by activation within and beyond the "Cyberball" brain network.

    “Reliving an unwanted rejection by a romantic partner was significantly characterized by activation within and beyond the "Cyberball" brain network, suggesting that the neural correlates of social pain are more complex than previously thought.”

    Meta-analysis, 2013. Cacioppo et al., Scientific Reports.

  3. Social rejection and physical pain share a common somatosensory representation in a network of brain regions that support the affective but not the sensory components of physical pain.

    “We demonstrate the overlap between social rejection and physical pain in these areas by comparing both conditions in the same individuals using functional MRI. ... They demonstrate that rejection and physical pain are similar not only in that they are both distressing--they share a common somatosensory representation as well.”

    Study, 2011. Kross et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

  4. Social pain relies on some of the same neural regions that process physical pain, but this is contested by theories that these responses reflect conflict detection, all negative affective processes, or the processing of salience.

    “Emerging evidence has shown that social pain--the painful feelings that follow from social rejection, exclusion, or loss--relies on some of the same neural regions that process physical pain, highlighting a possible physical-social pain overlap. However, the hypothesis that physical pain and social pain rely on shared neural systems has been contested.”

    Review, 2014. Eisenberger, Annual Review of Psychology.

  5. Recalling an experience of social or physical pain produces overlapping effects like thwarted self-esteem and control needs and increased negative affect and desire to aggress.

    “In Experiment 1, recalling an experience of social or physical pain produced overlapping effects in the form of thwarted self‐esteem and control needs and increased negative affect and desire to aggress.”

    Experiment, 2011. Riva et al., European Journal of Social Psychology.

  6. Inducing social ostracism or physical pain in a laboratory session makes people feel ignored and excluded and thwarts belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence.

    “In Experiment 2, we induced social (Cyberball ostracism) or physical pain (cold pressor) within the laboratory session, and found that both pain types produced feelings of being ignored and excluded, and thwarted belonging, self‐esteem, control, and meaningful existence.”

    Experiment, 2011. Riva et al., European Journal of Social Psychology.

Your brain processes the feeling, not just the sensation.

Social rejection and physical pain share a common somatosensory representation in regions that support the affective components of pain. They do not necessarily share the sensory components.

The ache is real but complex.

That may be why being shut out feels like a physical wound even if your body remains unharmed. Your brain interprets the loss of belonging as a threat to your existence.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The silence of a group chat.

You see the messages from the group. You see the names of people who were there. You are not mentioned. The screen stays blank. You feel the weight of the exclusion.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that social and physical pain share psychological outcomes and some brain regions, but significant research fails to confirm they use the same primary pain matrix.

The evidence is mixed because some studies find overlap while others suggest the neural responses to social pain are more complex or reflect different processes like salience or conflict detection.
