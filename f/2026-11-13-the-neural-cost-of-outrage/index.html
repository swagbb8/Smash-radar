Your brain is being hijacked

Is your outrage a survival instinct?

File no. 0276November 13, 2026Modern society

Your brain processes online outrage through prefrontal oscillations.

The evidence does not support the idea that online outrage is a biological survival threat linked to the amygdala. Instead, it involves complex neural patterns in the prefrontal cortex.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Negativity bias in digital feeds is a sequence involving attentional competition, moral appraisal and layered evaluation.

    “The findings of this study extend negativity bias from a final weighting effect to a sequence involving attentional competition, moral appraisal and layered evaluation.”

    Experiment, 2026. Hamelin & Naidoo, Journal of Product & Brand Management.

  2. Both semantic and response conflict were associated with mid-frontal theta-band and parietal alpha-band power modulations, irrespective of the level of conflict awareness.

    “We observed that both semantic as well as response conflict were associated with mid-frontal theta-band and parietal alpha-band power modulations, irrespective of the level of conflict awareness (high vs. low)”

    Experiment, 2015. Jiang et al., Scientific Reports.

  3. Stimulus prediction error was positively correlated with the power in the low-beta band (12-22 Hz) in the stop-locked interval.

    “Stimulus prediction error was positively correlated with the power in the low-beta band (12-22 Hz) in the stop-locked interval.”

    Study, 2017. Chang et al., eNeuro.

  4. Individual differences on the inclination to mitigate, the sentence reduction per unit of judged sympathy, correlated with activity in the right middle insula.

    “Individual differences on the inclination to mitigate, the sentence reduction per unit of judged sympathy, correlated with activity in the right middle insula, an area known to represent interoception of visceral states.”

    Study, 2012. Yamada et al., Nature Communications.

  5. Conflict detection is reflected in a DMPFC θ power increase that causally entrained DLPFC θ activity.

    “Subsequent conflict detection was reflected in a DMPFC θ power increase that causally entrained DLPFC θ activity (DMPFC to DLPFC).”

    Experiment, 2014. Oehrn et al., Journal of Neuroscience.

  6. Conflict resolution was completed by coupling of DLPFC γ power to DMPFC θ oscillations.

    “Conflict resolution was thereafter completed by coupling of DLPFC γ power to DMPFC θ oscillations.”

    Experiment, 2014. Oehrn et al., Journal of Neuroscience.

Conflict detection happens in the brain.

When you encounter conflicting information, your brain detects it. This detection is reflected in a specific power increase in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex.

Your reactions are a neural calculation.

That may be why you feel a physical shift when you see a moral conflict. Your brain is actively coupling different regions to resolve the tension.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The evening scroll.

You sit on your sofa at 11 p.m. A headline appears on your screen. You feel a sudden, sharp tension in your head before you even finish the first sentence.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that the brain uses specific prefrontal oscillations and visceral interoception to process conflict and moral appraisal, but it does not specifically link online outrage to amygdala-mediated survival threats.

None of the provided sources specifically measure amygdala activity or explicitly link digital conflict to biological survival threats.
