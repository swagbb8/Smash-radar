Do algorithms create a loop of bias?

File no. 0211November 1, 2026The future

The evidence on algorithmic bias is mixed.

While many assume these tools create racial feedback loops, a randomized trial found no significant differences in racial arrest proportions. However, evidence for their overall effectiveness remains weak.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. There were no significant differences in the proportion of arrests by racial-ethnic group between control and treatment conditions.

    “We find that there were no significant differences in the proportion of arrests by racial-ethnic group between control and treatment conditions.”

    Randomized trial, 2018. Brantingham et al., Statistics and Public Policy.

  2. When adjusted for the higher overall crime rate at algorithmically predicted locations, arrests were lower or unchanged.

    “When adjusted for the higher overall crime rate at algorithmically predicted locations, however, arrests were lower or unchanged.”

    Randomized trial, 2018. Brantingham et al., Statistics and Public Policy.

  3. Many police officers have a detailed awareness of the limitations of predictive technologies, specifically those brought about by errors and biases in input data.

    “Research findings show that many police officers have a detailed awareness of the limitations of predictive technologies, specifically those brought about by errors and biases in input data.”

    Study, 2020. Sandhu & Fussey, Policing & Society.

  4. The ambiguities and contradictions of the patrol are not resolved through algorithmic remediation.

    “The ambiguities and contradictions of the patrol are not resolved through algorithmic remediation.”

    Experiment, 2019. Shapiro, Surveillance & Society.

  5. Only 6 out of 161 articles were evidence-strong in the study's categorization of big data-driven predictive policing effectiveness.

    “we concluded that while it is encouraging to see a range of studies, given the significant concerns about big data-driven predictive policing, evidence of effectiveness that can be used by policymakers still needs to be supported by more research, as only 6 out of 161 were evidencestrong in our study categorisation.”

    Systematic review, 2024. Lee et al., Justice Evaluation Journal.

The data shows a different picture.

When researchers adjusted for higher crime rates at predicted locations, arrests were lower or unchanged. Police officers are aware of these data errors and input biases.

The tech doesn't solve the human problem.

Algorithmic remediation does not resolve the ambiguities and contradictions of patrol. That may be why the technology remains a tool rather than a solution.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The honest summary

The evidence does not confirm that these algorithms create feedback loops of over-policing, as the primary empirical study found no significant racial differences in arrest proportions.

The evidence for effectiveness is currently weak, with only 6 out of 161 reviewed articles providing strong evidence for policymakers.
