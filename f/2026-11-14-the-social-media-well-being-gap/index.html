Does scrolling actually change your mental health?

File no. 0280November 14, 2026Social media

The link between social media use and well-being is negligible.

Most results show an effect size of less than.10. The evidence is mixed and often contested.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Most studies did not support the idea that active social media use leads to higher well-being while passive social media use leads to lower well-being.

    “Most studies did not support the hypothesized associations of ASMU and PSMU with well-/ill-being.”

    Observational study, 2021. Valkenburg et al., New Media & Society. This shows a link, not a cause.

  2. Most of the results regarding the link between active or passive social media use and mental health were negligible, with an effect size of less than .10.

    “Most ESs were negligible (|r| < .10), with the exception of between-person associations for active SMU and greater online support (r = .34), wellbeing (r = .15), positive affect (r = .11), and symptoms of anxiety (r = .12), and passive SMU and greater online support (r = .15).”

    Meta-analysis, 2023. Godard & Holtzman, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication.

  3. Active social media use is linked to a negative relationship with appearance valence in physically active men.

    “Results showed a negative relationship between active social media use and appearance valence.”

    Study, 2024. Bell et al., New Media & Society.

  4. Time spent on active or passive social media use may be too coarse to lead to meaningful associations with well-being or ill-being.

    “Time spent on ASMU and PSMU may be too coarse to lead to meaningful associations with well-/ill-being.”

    Observational study, 2021. Valkenburg et al., New Media & Society. This shows a link, not a cause.

  5. Passive social media use is linked to worse emotional outcomes in general social media contexts, but not in social media groups.

    “Moderator analyses revealed that passive use was associated with worse emotional outcomes in general SM contexts, but not in the context of SM groups.”

    Meta-analysis, 2023. Godard & Holtzman, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication.

  6. Passive social media use is linked to higher appearance salience and drive for muscularity in physically active men.

    “Passive social media use was predictive of higher appearance salience and drive for muscularity in linear regression models.”

    Study, 2024. Bell et al., New Media & Society.

Context matters more than your mode of engagement.

Passive use is linked to worse emotional outcomes in general contexts but not in groups. Active use is linked to negative relationships with appearance valence in some men.

Time spent is an unreliable metric.

The time you spend on your phone may be too coarse to lead to meaningful associations with your mental state.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The honest summary

The evidence is mixed and often negligible, showing that the impact of social media use depends heavily on the specific context and the type of content rather than just the mode of engagement.

Many studies used 'time spent' as a metric, which may be too broad to provide meaningful insights into psychological outcomes.
