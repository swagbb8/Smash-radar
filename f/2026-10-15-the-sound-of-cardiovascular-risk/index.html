Does the city sound hurt your heart?

File no. 0090October 15, 2026Health and environment

Road noise is linked to cardiovascular risks.

Studies show a positive association between transportation noise and risks like arterial hypertension. However, some meta-analyses find the link to ischemic heart disease to be statistically insignificant.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. The association between transportation noise exposure and cardiovascular disease is evident, but not at a significant level.

    “It can be concluded that though the association between transportation noise exposure and cardiovascular disease is evident, but not at a significant level.”

    Meta-analysis, 2014. Banerjee, Indian Journal of Public Health.

  2. Road traffic noise [risk ratio (RR): 1. 03 (95% CI: 1. 02-1. 05) per 10 dB increase] is linked to increased risk of incident CVD development.

    “road traffic noise [risk ratio (RR): 1. 03 (95% CI: 1. 02-1. 05) per 10 dB increase] ... were each associated with increased risk of incident CVD development.”

    Meta-analysis, 2025. Brown et al., European Journal of Preventive Cardiology.

The evidence is mixed.

While some data links road traffic noise to incident cardiovascular disease, the strength and significance of these connections vary. The quality of evidence remains low or inconsistent across different heart diseases.

The environment shapes your body.

That may be why your physical health is tied to the sounds of the city. Your heart reacts to the constant flow of the infrastructure around you.

The hum of the morning.

You lie in bed while the steady drone of tires on wet pavement vibrates through the walls. It is a constant presence you have learned to ignore.

The honest summary

The evidence shows a positive association between road traffic noise and cardiovascular risks like hypertension and incident CVD, though the strength and significance of these links vary across different studies.

The quality of evidence for many associations remains low or inconsistent, with some studies failing to reach statistical significance for specific heart diseases.
