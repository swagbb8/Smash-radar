Your brain is being hijacked

Does hearing a lie enough times make it true?

File no. 0033October 8, 2026Psychology

Repetition does not make opinions feel true.

While repeated lies feel more factual, this effect does not apply to social-political opinions.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Prior knowledge does not protect against the illusory truth effect, even when the repeated false statement contradicts what a person already knows.

    “We again find that prior knowledge does not protect against the illusory truth effect. Repeated false statements were given higher truth ratings than novel statements, even when they contradicted participants’ prior knowledge.”

    Experiment, 2020. Fazio, Collabra Psychology.

  2. People sometimes rely on fluency even if they already have knowledge available to them.

    “Multinomial modeling demonstrated that participants sometimes rely on fluency even if knowledge is also available to them (Experiment 2).”

    Experiment, 2015. Fazio et al., Journal of experimental psychology. General.

  3. The illusory truth effect occurs even when repetitions happen minutes or weeks apart.

    “Contrary to some theoretical predictions, the illusory truth effect seems to be similar in magnitude for repetitions occurring after minutes or weeks.”

    Experiment, 2021. Henderson et al., Journal of Cognition.

  4. Repetition does not significantly increase the perceived truthfulness of social-political opinion statements.

    “A mini meta-analysis combining the present findings with prior work further supported the conclusion that repetition effects for opinion statements are negligible within the tested bounds.”

    Meta-analysis, 2026. Riesthuis & Woods, Consciousness and cognition.

  5. Repetition of misinformation makes people more likely to share it because it biases their judgment of accuracy.

    “the relationship between repetition and sharing was mediated by perceived accuracy. That is, repetition of misinformation biased people's judgment of accuracy and as a result fuelled the spread of misinformation.”

    Experiment, 2023. Vellani et al., Cognition.

  6. The largest increase in perceived truth comes from encountering a statement for the second time, with smaller increases for each additional repetition.

    “The largest increase in perceived truth came from encountering a statement for the second time, and beyond this were incrementally smaller increases in perceived truth for each additional repetition.”

    Experiment, 2021. Hassan & Barber, Cognitive Research Principles and Implications.

Fluency creates a false sense of truth.

Your brain relies on fluency even when you already have knowledge. Repetition makes a statement feel familiar, which your brain interprets as accuracy.

The limits of influence.

That may be why you are less likely to be swayed by a repeated political slogan than by a repeated lie about a physical fact.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A scroll through your feed.

You see a fake news story about a chemical. You see it again an hour later. You start to believe it is real.

The honest summary

Repetition increases perceived truthfulness for factual information by leveraging fluency, but this effect is limited by the type of content being presented.

The illusory truth effect may be stronger over shorter timescales and is notably less effective for evaluative or subjective opinions.
