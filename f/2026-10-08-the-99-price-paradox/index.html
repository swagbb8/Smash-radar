Your brain is being hijacked

You think the leftmost digit matters. The evidence is mixed.

File no. 0052October 8, 2026Money and power

Evidence on the.

Some studies show neurological changes in the brain, while large-scale online experiments found no support for the left-digit effect.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. An online experiment with 266 participants making 4788 purchasing decisions found no support for the left-digit effect.

    “Against expectations, we found no support for either of the two price-perception effects.”

    Experiment, 2022. Fenneman et al., PLoS ONE.

  2. An empirical study in Germany found that psychological factors tested in different experiments have a relatively low impact on the assessment of perceived value for money and cheapness.

    “it turns out that the assessment of perceived value for money and cheapness for different sales promotion campaigns in Germany (train ticket, smartphone flat rate, filter coffee) is amazingly robust and that psychological factors tested in different experiments have a relatively low impact.”

    Experiment, 2015. Krämer, Business and Management Studies.

  3. A change in the leftmost digit causes a decrease in brain activity related to visuospatial processing, which may lead to an underestimation of the payment amount.

    “Our results suggested that a decrease in activity in the regions associated with visuospatial processing suggests that changes in the leftmost digits alter the level of visuospatial attention towards the price, leading to an underestimation of the payment amount.”

    Experiment, 2025. Ogata & Sugiura, Biological psychology.

  4. An experiment showed that when consumers view regular and sale prices with identical left digits, they perceive larger price discounts when the right digits are less than 5.

    “Consistent with our hypothesized right digit effect, we find that, when consumers view regular and sale prices with identical left digits, they perceive larger price discounts when the right digits are “small” (i.e., less than 5) than when they are “large” (i.e., greater than 5).”

    Experiment, 2007. Coulter & Coulter, Journal of Consumer Research.

  5. A new experiment showed that assuming the first digit of the price is memorized, a round starting price leads to an overestimation of the amount of the offered discount.

    “Assuming the first digit of the price is memorized, we might expect that a round starting price leads to an overestimation of the amount of the offered discount. The results provide evidence in support of this hypothesis”

    Experiment, 2004. Guéguen & Legohérel, European Journal of Marketing.

The data varies by study type.

Small experiments show changes in brain activity and perceived discounts. However, real-world assessments and large online experiments show these effects are often non-existent or have a low impact.

Your perception may be inconsistent.

That may be why you feel a price change is significant in some moments but find it irrelevant in others. Your brain might react, but your wallet may not always follow.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A shelf in a grocery store.

You stand in the aisle. You look at a box of cereal. You see the price tag. You notice the digits on the right side of the decimal point.

The honest summary

The evidence is mixed, with some studies showing neurological and cognitive effects of the left-digit while others find no significant impact on actual purchasing decisions.

The results vary significantly between small-scale experimental designs and larger online experiments or real-world assessments.
