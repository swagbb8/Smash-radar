Does watching eyes make you a better person?

File no. 0202October 30, 2026Psychology

Eyes on a wall do not make you more generous.

While eye cues reduce antisocial behavior, they do not influence how much you donate. The evidence is mixed.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. Two meta-analyses on the effect, which focused on generosity measured by donations and economic games, suggest that artificial surveillance cues do not influence the donation amount.

    “Two meta-analyses on the effect, which focused on generosity measured by donations and economic games, suggest that artificial surveillance cues do not influence the donation amount.”

    Meta-analysis, 2019. Oda, Letters on Evolutionary Behavioral Science.

  2. Women give more than men in South America, North America and Oceania, while they give less than men in South Africa.

    “On the other hand, women give more than men in South America, North America and Oceania, while they give less than men in South Africa.”

    Meta-analysis, 2022. Doñate-Buendía et al., Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization.

  3. A meta-analysis of 15 experiments involving 2035 participants shows that photographs and/or stylized images of eyes reduced antisocial behaviour by 35%.

    “Our meta-analysis of 15 experiments involving 2035 participants shows that photographs and/or stylized images of eyes reduced antisocial behaviour by 35%.”

    Meta-analysis, 2019. Dear et al., Evolution and Human Behavior.

  4. Women are on average significantly more generous than men, even if controlling for several moderator variables that are included in the study.

    “It is found that gender differences exist, being women on average significantly more generous than men, even if controlling for several moderator variables that are included in the study.”

    Meta-analysis, 2022. Doñate-Buendía et al., Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization.

The effect of surveillance is inconsistent.

Research shows that stylized images of eyes can reduce antisocial behavior by 35%. However, these same cues do not change donation amounts in economic games.

Presence matters more than imagery.

The number of people around you during a decision may be a more significant factor than the visual cues on the wall. This may be why your behavior feels unpredictable.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The donation box in the lobby.

You walk past a jar for charity. A poster with eyes watches you. You decide whether or not to reach into your pocket.

The honest summary

The evidence is contradictory, showing that eye cues may reduce antisocial behavior but do not necessarily increase generosity in donation-based settings.

The number of people around when a donation decision is made is identified as a potentially important factor that can bias results.
