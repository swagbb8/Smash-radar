Do you choose your actions, or do they happen to you?

File no. 0163October 24, 2026Science and reality

Neural signals before your choice are not the cause of it.

Research shows that the readiness potential is unrelated to your conscious intentions and does not cause you to act.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. Neither the RP nor the LRP index neural processes are causal of conscious willing.

    “neither the RP nor the LRP index neural processes are causal of conscious willing.”

    Experiment, 2014. Alexander et al., Oxford University Press eBooks.

  2. Both the RP and LRP potentials seem unrelated to conscious intentions to move.

    “Both potentials seem unrelated to conscious intentions to move.”

    Experiment, 2014. Alexander et al., Oxford University Press eBooks.

  3. The signal accumulation in the Veto Libet task would be less steep than in the Classical Libet task, resulting in longer wait times and earlier self-reported intentions to act.

    “We hypothesized that the signal accumulation in the Veto Libet task would be less steep than in the Classical Libet task, resulting in longer wait times and earlier self-reported intentions to act (i. e. , the W).”

    Experiment, 2024. Shum et al., Consciousness and cognition.

  4. The readiness potential (RP) does not reflect uniquely motor-related processing.

    “the RP does not reflect uniquely motor-related processing, while the lateralized readiness potential (LRP) likely does”

    Experiment, 2014. Alexander et al., Oxford University Press eBooks.

  5. Recent advances in understanding the RP call for a reassessment of its relevance for understanding volition and the philosophical problem of free will.

    “recent advances in our understanding of the RP, including computational modeling of the phenomenon, call for a reassessment of its relevance for understanding volition and the philosophical problem of free will.”

    Review, 2021. Schurger et al., Trends in Cognitive Sciences.

  6. The link between the Libet paradigm and free will or moral responsibility is not clear.

    “While the Libet paradigm may be useful for examining how stimuli affect temporal judgments, the link between this and free will or moral responsibility is not clear.”

    Experiment, 2018. Saigle et al., AJOB Neuroscience.

The brain prepares before you decide.

The readiness potential does not reflect uniquely motor-related processing. Because of this, the link between these neural signals and free will remains unclear.

The origin of your will is unknown.

That may be why your choices feel like your own even if the underlying mechanics are different from your conscious experience.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The coffee cup.

You reach for your mug in the morning. Your hand moves before you feel the command to pick it up. You see the steam rise first, then feel the weight in your palm.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that the readiness potential is not a reliable indicator of conscious will or the causal mechanism for it.

The link between neural signals and abstract concepts like free will or moral responsibility remains unclear and subject to ongoing critique.
