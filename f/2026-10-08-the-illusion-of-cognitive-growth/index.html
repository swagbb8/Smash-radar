Can a game actually make you smarter?

File no. 0049October 8, 2026The human brain

Your brain gets better at the game, not your life.

Training produces improvements in specific skills like working memory, but these gains rarely transfer to general intelligence or real-world abilities. This evidence is mixed regarding the scale of these effects.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Studies with brain injured patients demonstrated long-lasting improvements in WM functions with moderate to large effect sizes.

    “Studies with brain injured patients demonstrated long-lasting improvements in WM functions with moderate to large effect sizes.”

    Meta-analysis, 2015. Weicker et al., Neuropsychology.

  2. Far-transfer effects were smaller than near-transfer effects but were significant for the net pretest-to-posttest gain relative to passive control groups and for the net gain at posttest relative to both active and passive control groups.

    “Far-transfer effects were smaller than near-transfer effects but were significant for the net pretest-to-posttest gain relative to passive control groups and for the net gain at posttest relative to both active and passive control groups.”

    Meta-analysis, 2014. Karbach & Verhaeghen, Psychological Science.

  3. Working memory training produces short-term, specific training effects that do not generalize to measures of 'real-world' cognitive skills.

    “The authors conclude that working memory training programs appear to produce short-term, specific training effects that do not generalize to measures of "real-world" cognitive skills.”

    Meta-analysis, 2016. Melby‐Lervåg et al., Perspectives on Psychological Science.

  4. Far-transfer effects to other cognitive skills were small, limited to nonverbal (g = 0.14) and verbal (g = 0.16) ability and not sustained at follow-up.

    “Far-transfer effects to other cognitive skills were small, limited to nonverbal (g = 0.14) and verbal (g = 0.16) ability and not sustained at follow-up.”

    Meta-analysis, 2015. Schwaighofer et al., Educational Psychologist.

  5. Working memory training exerted a significant effect on cognitive skills related to WM training (g = 0.46), little evidence was found regarding far-transfer effects (g = 0.12).

    “Whereas WM training exerted a significant effect on cognitive skills related to WM training (g = 0. 46), little evidence was found regarding far-transfer effects (g = 0. 12).”

    Meta-analysis, 2017. Sala & Gobet, Developmental Psychology.

Near-transfer is the only consistent result.

Research shows that training produces short-term, specific effects. Gains in verbal and nonverbal abilities are small and often do not last at follow-up.

The illusion of progress.

That may be why you feel sharper after a session. You are practicing a specific task, but your ability to navigate your daily life remains unchanged.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The evening routine.

You sit on your sofa at 9 pm. You tap the screen to solve puzzles. You feel more capable, then you put the phone down and struggle to remember your keys in the morning fog. You are still you, just with a slightly faster reaction to a digital task.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that training produces 'near-transfer' improvements in the specific skills being practiced, but 'far-transfer' to general cognitive abilities is consistently small or non-existent.

The effectiveness of training often depends on the quality of the experimental design, such as the presence of active control groups.

Are you training your brain or just training your habit?
