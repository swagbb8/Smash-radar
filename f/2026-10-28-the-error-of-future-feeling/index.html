The uncomfortable truth

Why does your fear of the future feel so heavy?

File no. 0188October 28, 2026Psychology

You are bad at predicting your own feelings.

You consistently overestimate how intense and how long your emotional reactions will be to future events.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence backs this.

What the research found

  1. Forecasters underestimated the hedonic impact of winning a smaller prize that they expected to win.

    “In Experiment 1, affective forecasters underestimated the hedonic impact of winning a smaller prize that they expected to win”

    Experiment, 2017. Buechel et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology General.

  2. Forecasters overestimated the hedonic impact of winning a larger prize that they did not expect to win.

    “and they overestimated the hedonic impact of winning a larger prize that they did not expect to win.”

    Experiment, 2017. Buechel et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology General.

  3. People fail to predict how quickly they will cope with negative events in ways that speed up their emotional recovery.

    “People fail to anticipate how quickly they will cope psychologically with such events in ways that speed their recovery from them.”

    Study, 2005. Wilson & Gilbert, Current Directions in Psychological Science.

  4. Affective forecasting errors happen because people underestimate how much other events will influence their thoughts and feelings.

    “One cause of the impact bias is focalism, the tendency to underestimate the extent to which other events will influence our thoughts and feelings.”

    Study, 2005. Wilson & Gilbert, Current Directions in Psychological Science.

  5. Affective forecasting interventions changed anticipated regret by d = 0.24, 95% confidence interval (CI) (0.15, 0.32), p < .05.

    “Overall, affective forecasting interventions changed anticipated regret, d = 0.24, 95% confidence interval (CI) (0.15, 0.32), p < .05.”

    Meta-analysis, 2018. Ellis et al., Annals of Behavioral Medicine.

  6. People often overestimate how intense and how long their emotional reactions will be to future events.

    “They often display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to such events.”

    Study, 2005. Wilson & Gilbert, Current Directions in Psychological Science.

You fail to see your own resilience.

You underestimate how quickly you will cope with negative events. You also fail to account for how other life events will influence your thoughts and feelings.

Your fear is a calculation error.

That may be why your anxiety about future events feels heavier than it will actually be. You are ignoring your own ability to adapt and the noise of other life events.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The weight of a new job.

You sit at your desk on a Sunday night, imagining the crushing stress of Monday morning. You feel the weight of it now, but it will feel different by Tuesday afternoon. You will have already adjusted to the new routine, the new names, and the new tasks. The peak of your fear will pass faster than you think it will. You will simply keep going, finding a way to fit the change into your daily life without the intensity you fear today.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that people consistently overestimate the emotional impact of future events, though the magnitude of these errors can vary based on the specific nature of the event.

The sources do not provide a specific timeline for how long these emotional impacts actually last, only that they are typically overestimated.
