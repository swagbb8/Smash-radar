Does your smoothie actually lower your Glycemic Index?

File no. 0330November 25, 2026Health and environment

Extracting nutrients from certain fruits can lower their Glycemic Index.

While smoothies require more meals to maintain a low Glycemic Load, the process of nutrient extraction in specific fruits like raspberries and passionfruit can actually reduce their Glycemic Index.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. Nutrient extraction of seeded fruits reduces the Glycemic Index compared to eating fruit whole in subjects with obesity.

    “Nutrient extraction was shown to significantly lower GI, compared to eating fruit whole, in subjects with obesity (raspberry/mango: 25.43 ± 18.20 vs. 44.85 ± 20.18, p = 0.034 and passion fruit/mango (26.30 ± 25.72 vs. 42.56 ± 20.64, p = 0.044).”

    Clinical trial, 2020. Alkutbe et al., Nutrients.

  2. Smoothies had a higher median number of meals (12.04) to reach an acceptable Glycemic Load of less than 20 compared to high-school (5.75) or fast-food (4.48) diets.

    “Mexican diets were the common predictor for GI and carbohydrates per meal to reach acceptable GL < 20; with smoothies (12.04), high-school (5.75), fast-food (4.48), Korean (4.30), Chinese (3.93), and liquid diets (3.71) presenting a higher median number of meals.”

    Study, 2023. Chen et al., Nutrients.

  3. Nutrient extraction of raspberries and passionfruit mixed with mango lowers the Glycemic Index in healthy weight individuals.

    “Similar results were found in those of a healthy weight.”

    Clinical trial, 2020. Alkutbe et al., Nutrients.

  4. Diets with more carbohydrates and more meals were needed to reach an acceptable Glycemic Load of less than 20, including smoothies and liquid diets.

    “Diets with greater carbohydrates and more meals needed to reach acceptable GL < 20 included smoothies, convenient diets, and liquids.”

    Study, 2023. Chen et al., Nutrients.

The evidence on smoothies is mixed.

Smoothies and liquid diets require more meals to reach an acceptable Glycemic Load of less than 20. However, nutrient extraction of specific fruits lowers the Glycemic Index in some individuals.

Your dietary goals depend on the specific fruit.

That may be why a smoothie does not behave like a whole fruit. The impact on your blood sugar depends on whether the extraction process helps or hinders the Glycemic Index.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The morning routine.

You pour the blended fruit into a glass. You drink it quickly before starting your day. You assume it is the healthiest choice for your blood sugar levels.

The honest summary

The evidence contradicts the proposed angle by showing that nutrient extraction can lower the Glycemic Index of certain fruits, even though smoothies are associated with higher meal counts to maintain a low Glycemic Load.

The study on nutrient extraction specifically looked at raspberries, mango, and passion fruit rather than a general analysis of all blended fruits and vegetables.
