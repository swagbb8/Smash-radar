The uncomfortable truth

Does watching others make you feel less?

File no. 0083October 14, 2026Social media

Passive scrolling causes declines in well-being by increasing envy.

While general social media use shows inconsistent links to mental health, specifically watching others without interacting is associated with lower affective well-being.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Most studies did not support the hypothesized associations of active and passive social media use with well-being.

    “Most studies did not support the hypothesized associations of ASMU and PSMU with well-/ill-being.”

    Observational study, 2021. Valkenburg et al., New Media & Society.

  2. Passive Facebook usage causes declines in affective well-being over time by increasing envy.

    “It also demonstrated how passive Facebook usage leads to declines in affective well-being: by increasing envy.”

    Experiment, 2015. Verduyn et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology General.

  3. Active social media use is linked to greater online support (r = .34), wellbeing (r = .15), positive affect (r = .11), and symptoms of anxiety (r = .12).

    “Most ESs were negligible (|r| < .10), with the exception of between-person associations for active SMU and greater online support (r = .34), wellbeing (r = .15), positive affect (r = .11), and symptoms of anxiety (r = .12)”

    Meta-analysis, 2023. Godard & Holtzman, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication.

  4. Taking a break from Facebook causes life satisfaction to increase and emotions to become more positive, especially for passive Facebook users.

    “it was demonstrated that taking a break from Facebook has positive effects on the two dimensions of well-being: our life satisfaction increases and our emotions become more positive. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that these effects were significantly greater for heavy Facebook users, passive Facebook users, and users who tend to envy others on Facebook.”

    Experiment, 2016. Tromholt, Cyberpsychology Behavior and Social Networking.

  5. Passive social media use is linked to greater online support (r = .15).

    “and passive SMU and greater online support (r = .15).”

    Meta-analysis, 2023. Godard & Holtzman, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication.

  6. Social media comparison (r=−0.30) and problematic social media use (r=−0.13) are linked to lower positive well-being.

    “Conversely, social media comparison (r=−0. 30) and problematic social media use (r=−0. 13) showed negative correlations with positive well-being.”

    Meta-analysis, 2024. Marciano et al., SSM - Mental Health.

The evidence is mixed regarding social media and mental health.

Most studies do not support hypothesized associations between social media use and well-being. However, passive use specifically harms well-being through envy, while active use is linked to both support and anxiety.

The way you use the screen changes your internal state.

That may be why taking a break from Facebook leads to increased life satisfaction and more positive emotions, particularly for those who primarily watch others.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that passive use can specifically harm well-being via envy, but the overall relationship between social media types and mental health is inconsistent across different research methods.

Many studies found that the amount of time spent on social media may be too coarse a metric to lead to meaningful associations with well-being.
