Does capturing a moment erase it?

File no. 0120October 18, 2026Technology

Taking photos makes you remember more of the surroundings.

While you forget the specific objects you capture, you recognize nonphotographed aspects of the experience better than those without a camera.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Taking photos makes people recognize even nonphotographed aspects better than people without a camera.

    “Furthermore, participants who used a camera during their experience recognized even nonphotographed aspects better than participants without a camera did.”

    Study, 2017. Barasch et al., Psychological Science.

  2. Taking photos makes people remember more of what they saw and less of what they heard compared to those who could not take photographs.

    “we found that, even without revisiting any photos, participants who could freely take photographs during an experience recognized more of what they saw and less of what they heard, compared with those who could not take any photographs.”

    Study, 2017. Barasch et al., Psychological Science.

  3. Taking photos makes people perform worse on both perceptually driven and conceptually driven memory tests.

    “Contrary to predictions, the results showed impairment both on perceptually driven and conceptually driven tests, which were found both after a short (20 min) and long (48 h) delay between encoding at test.”

    Experiment, 2021. Lurie & Westerman, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition.

  4. Taking photos makes people less likely to remember objects they photograph than objects they only observe.

    “A photo-taking-impairment effect has been observed such that participants are less likely to remember objects they photograph than objects they only observe.”

    Experiment, 2017. Soares & Storm, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition.

  5. Taking photos makes people less accurate and less confident in remembering artworks during a mock museum tour.

    “In Experiment 1 and 3 following intentional encoding, a photo-taking impairment effect impacting accuracy and confidence was observed.”

    Experiment, 2024. Yaya et al., Journal of Cognitive Psychology.

  6. Taking photos makes people perform worse on on-slide information in an in-person learning environment.

    “Experiment 2 failed to replicate this interaction; there was an overall photo-taking impairment for on-slide information in an in-person learning environment.”

    Experiment, 2025. Trego et al., Behavioral sciences (Basel, Switzerland).

The camera changes how your brain records data.

Taking photos makes you remember more of what you see but less of what you hear. It makes you perform worse on both perceptually and conceptually driven memory tests.

The camera creates a trade-off in your memory.

That may be why you can recall the layout of a room but cannot remember the items on the table. Your brain offloads the specific detail to the device.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A walk through a museum at dusk.

You hold your phone out to capture a painting. You remember the light in the hall, but the details of the canvas fade.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that photo-taking typically impairs memory for specific items but can simultaneously enhance visual recognition and memory for surrounding context.

The effects of photo-taking vary significantly depending on the environment, such as whether the learning is happening in-person or online.
