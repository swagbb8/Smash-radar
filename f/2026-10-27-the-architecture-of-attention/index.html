Your brain is being hijacked

Does your phone choose what you see first?

File no. 0184October 27, 2026Modern society

Your brain releases dopamine when you receive alerts.

Neuroimaging tests confirm that participants experience a dopamine response when they receive alerts. The evidence on how these cues hijack attention is currently emerging and has not been peer-reviewed.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Modern digital cues can hijack attentional resources even when they are not personally relevant.

    “Together, our results show that modern digital cues can hijack attentional resources, even in the absence of explicit personal relevance.”

    Preprint, not peer reviewed, 2025. Fournier et al..

  2. Technology companies use psychological cues to design digital experiences that capture people's attention.

    “Our study showed that technology companies employ psychological cues to design digital experiences that capture individuals' attention.”

    Study, 2025. D, INTERANTIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH IN ENGINEERING AND MANAGEMENT.

  3. Social media notifications are linked to changes in pupil dilation, which is a physiological marker of cognitive load.

    “These findings were mirrored in changes in pupil dilation, a physiological marker of cognitive load, confirming the convergent impact of notification relevance and smartphone habits.”

    Preprint, not peer reviewed, 2025. Fournier et al..

  4. Neuroimaging tests confirmed that participants experienced a dopamine response when they received alerts.

    “Neuroimaging tests confirmed the dopamine response participants experienced when they received alerts, which is why it is hard to change these habits.”

    Study, 2025. D, INTERANTIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH IN ENGINEERING AND MANAGEMENT.

  5. 29.8% of students and working professionals reported they could not focus on their work or engage in proper interactions.

    “29. 8% of students and working professionals reported they could not focus on their work or engage in proper interactions.”

    Study, 2025. D, INTERANTIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENTIFIC RESEARCH IN ENGINEERING AND MANAGEMENT.

  6. A study of Indians between the ages of 15 to 35 found a moderate to severe case of nomophobia.

    “The purpose of this study is to ascertain the degree of nomophobia among Indians between the ages of 15 to 35. ... there is a serious concern for our "physical, mental, and social health" and a moderate to severe case of nomophobia”

    Observational study, 2024. Dr.Thangavel, Current Trends in Mass Communication.

Digital cues are designed to capture your attention.

Technology companies use psychological cues to create experiences that capture your focus. These notifications can hijack your attentional resources even when the information is not personally relevant to you.

Your focus is being diverted.

The constant stream of notifications may be why you find it difficult to engage in proper interactions or stay focused on your work throughout the day.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The steady hum of your phone on a desk.

You sit down to write a report. A small light flashes or a tone sounds. You look at the screen. You look back at your work, but your focus is gone.

The honest summary

The sources confirm that notifications trigger dopamine and hijack attention, but they do not explicitly link these mechanisms to gambling-style variable reward schedules.

One source regarding the hijacking of attention is a preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.
