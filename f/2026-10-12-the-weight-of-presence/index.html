Does your phone affect you when it is silent?

File no. 0073October 12, 2026Technology

The mere presence of a smartphone lowers your performance.

You do not have to touch it or look at it. Just having the device within reach reduces your cognitive ability.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence backs this.

What the research found

  1. The mere presence of a smartphone results in lower cognitive performance.

    “The results of the conducted experiment imply that the mere presence of a smartphone results in lower cognitive performance, which supports the hypothesis of the smartphone presence using limited cognitive resources.”

    Experiment, 2023. Skowronek et al., Scientific Reports.

  2. Interruptions coming early during a search task are more likely to result in the user forgetting the primary task goal than interruptions that arrive later on.

    “In addition, we show that interruptions coming early during a search task are more likely to result in the user forgetting the primary task goal than interruptions that arrive later on.”

    Study, 2001. Cutrell et al.. This shows a link, not a cause.

  3. There is a general tendency to resume tasks, but no memory advantage for unfinished tasks.

    “We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks.”

    Meta-analysis, 2025. Ghibellini & Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications.

  4. Notifications are more disruptive for fast, stimulus-driven search tasks than for slower, more effortful semantic-based search tasks.

    “Our findings replicate earlier work, showing the generally harmful effects of IM, and further show that notifications are more disruptive for fast, stimulus-driven search tasks than for slower, more effortful semantic-based search tasks.”

    Study, 2000. Czerwinski et al..

  5. When the primary task was considered difficult, subjects forced to multitask had significantly lower performance compared with not only the subjects who did not multitask but also the subjects who were able to multitask at their discretion.

    “The results show that when the primary task was considered difficult, subjects forced to multitask had significantly lower performance compared with not only the subjects who did not multitask but also the subjects who were able to multitask at their discretion.”

    Experiment, 2014. Adler & Benbunan‐Fich, Interacting with Computers.

  6. Reducing notification-caused interruptions is beneficial for performance and reducing strain.

    “The findings revealed that a reduction of notification-caused interruptions is beneficial for performance and reducing strain.”

    Experiment, 2023. Ohly & Bastin, Journal of Occupational Health.

Your task difficulty determines the impact.

Notifications disrupt fast, stimulus-driven searches more than slow, effortful tasks. However, when a task is difficult, forced multitasking leads to significantly lower performance.

The timing of a notification matters.

Interruptions early in a search task are linked to forgetting the primary goal. That may be why your focus feels fractured the moment you start something new.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A phone sits on the desk next to your laptop.

You are trying to write a report. The screen stays dark. You still feel your concentration thinning as you glance toward it.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that notifications and the presence of smartphones disrupt cognitive performance and memory, though the severity depends on the specific nature of the task being performed.

The research does not fully explain the specific role of anxiety in impeding performance when notifications are disabled.
