Why do you believe what you see most often?

File no. 0134October 20, 2026Social media

Exposure to longer misinformation creates a stronger belief in its truth.

The longer you are exposed to a false statement, the more likely you are to accept it as fact. This effect happens regardless of your cognitive ability or thinking style.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence backs this.

What the research found

  1. The illusory truth effect is not changed by a person's cognitive ability, need for cognitive closure, or cognitive style.

    “These results indicate that the illusory truth effect is robust to individual differences in cognitive ability, need for cognitive closure, and cognitive style.”

    Study, 2019. keersmaecker et al., Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.

  2. Beliefs can influence learning success through top-down biasing of attention and conceptual processing toward goal-congruent information.

    “These results suggest that beliefs can influence learning success through top-down biasing of attention and conceptual processing toward goal-congruent information.”

    Study, 2006. Mangels et al., Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience.

  3. Exposure to health misinformation, on average, increases individuals’ belief in misinformation, g = 0.24, 95% Confidence Interval [0.10, 0.38].

    “the meta-analysis examined 31 effect sizes (N = 18,115) from 14 studies that showed that exposure to health misinformation, on average, increases individuals’ belief in misinformation, g = 0. 24, 95% Confidence Interval [0. 10, 0. 38].”

    Meta-analysis, 2025. Schmid & Bauer, Health Communication.

  4. Exposure to longer health misinformation is linked to stronger effects, g = 0.41, 95% CI [0.27, 0.55], than exposure to shorter statements, g = 0.06, 95% CI [-0.08, 0.20].

    “Moderator analyses revealed that exposure to longer health misinformation was associated with stronger effects, g = 0. 41, 95% CI [0. 27, 0. 55], than exposure to shorter statements, g = 0. 06, 95% CI [−0. 08, 0. 20].”

    Meta-analysis, 2025. Schmid & Bauer, Health Communication.

  5. The ease of processing induces positive judgments in a wide variety of tasks.

    “It is known that the ease of processing induces positive judgments in a wide variety of tasks (the fluency effect).”

    Review, 2023. Yagi et al., The Japanese journal of psychology.

  6. People are more likely to believe information is true if they have encountered it before.

    “People are more inclined to believe that information is true if they have encountered it before.”

    Study, 2019. keersmaecker et al., Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.

Familiarity creates a sense of truth.

Your brain finds familiar information easier to process. This ease of processing induces positive judgments. You are more likely to believe something is true simply because you have encountered it before.

Your preferences are being shaped by repetition.

That may be why you find yourself agreeing with ideas you first heard as false. Repeated contact with misinformation creates a bias in how your brain processes information.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The same phrase on a scrolling feed.

You see a specific health claim on your phone. You see it again the next day. By the third time, it feels like common sense.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that familiarity with information increases its perceived truthfulness, particularly when the exposure is prolonged.

While exposure increases belief, the meta-analysis of health misinformation showed mixed results regarding the persuasive impact of such exposure.
