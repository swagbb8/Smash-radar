Does predictive policing actually create racial bias?

File no. 0232November 4, 2026Technology

Predictive policing does not show significant racial bias in arrests.

A randomized trial found no significant differences in the proportion of arrests by racial-ethnic group during these deployments.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. There were no significant differences in the proportion of arrests by racial-ethnic group between control and treatment conditions.

    “We find that there were no significant differences in the proportion of arrests by racial-ethnic group between control and treatment conditions.”

    Randomized trial, 2018. Brantingham et al., Statistics and Public Policy.

  2. When adjusted for the higher overall crime rate at algorithmically predicted locations, arrests were lower or unchanged.

    “When adjusted for the higher overall crime rate at algorithmically predicted locations, however, arrests were lower or unchanged.”

    Randomized trial, 2018. Brantingham et al., Statistics and Public Policy.

  3. Only 6 out of 161 articles were evidence-strong in the study's categorization of big data-driven predictive policing effectiveness.

    “evidence of effectiveness that can be used by policymakers still needs to be supported by more research, as only 6 out of 161 were evidencestrong in our study categorisation.”

    Systematic review, 2024. Lee et al., Justice Evaluation Journal.

  4. Predictive policing for reform is operationalized as a series of technical fixes that rely on the production of indeterminacies.

    “Understanding how the “predictive policing for reform” discourse is operationalized as a series of technical fixes that rely on the production of indeterminacies allows for a more nuanced critique of predictive policing.”

    Experiment, 2019. Shapiro, Surveillance & Society.

  5. Reported incidents of crime can attenuate the degree of runaway feedback, but they cannot entirely remove it without specific interventions.

    “in brief, while reported incidents can attenuate the degree of runaway feedback, they cannot entirely remove it without the interventions we suggest.”

    Study, 2018. Ensign et al..

The evidence on these tools is mixed.

While runaway feedback loops are a theoretical risk, empirical data from a randomized trial did not show significant racial bias. Only 6 out of 161 reviewed articles were considered evidence-strong.

Technical fixes may mask deeper issues.

Predictive policing for reform is often operationalized as a series of technical fixes that rely on the production of indeterminacies. That may be why the results are so difficult to interpret.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The honest summary

The evidence is mixed, showing that while runaway feedback loops are a theoretical and modeled risk, empirical data from a randomized trial did not show significant racial bias in arrests.

The evidence for the effectiveness of these tools is currently limited, with only 6 out of 161 reviewed articles being considered evidence-strong.
