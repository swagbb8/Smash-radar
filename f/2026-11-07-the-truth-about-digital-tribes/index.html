Does talking to your tribe make you more biased?

File no. 0248November 7, 2026The future

Social interaction in networks can reduce polarization.

Evidence shows that sharing opinions in structured online networks can improve accuracy and decrease bias among people with similar views.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. Social information exchange in homogeneous networks not only increased accuracy but also reduced polarization.

    “In contrast to polarization theories, we found that social information exchange in homogeneous networks not only increased accuracy but also reduced polarization.”

    Experiment, 2019. Becker et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America.

  2. Smokers and nonsmokers significantly improved in their ability to accurately interpret anti-smoking messages by sharing their opinions in structured online social networks.

    “However, smokers and nonsmokers significantly improved in their ability to accurately interpret anti-smoking messages by sharing their opinions in structured online social networks.”

    Experiment, 2020. Guilbeault & Centola, PLoS ONE.

  3. The filter bubbles in progressive media were not as strong as those in conservative media.

    “The filter bubbles in progressive media were not as strong as those in conservative media.”

    Study, 2024. Park & Park, Asian Journal of Communication.

  4. Polarized communities emerge around distinct types of contents and usual consumers of conspiracy news result to be more focused and self-contained on their specific contents.

    “Our results show that polarized communities emerge around distinct types of contents and usual consumers of conspiracy news result to be more focused and self-contained on their specific contents.”

    Study, 2015. Bessi et al., PLoS ONE.

  5. More than 30% of the videos recommended by progressive media were from legacy media, while this figure was 25% for conservative sources.

    “More than 30% of the videos recommended by progressive media were from legacy media, while this figure was 25% for conservative sources.”

    Study, 2024. Park & Park, Asian Journal of Communication.

The evidence on network behavior is mixed.

While filter bubbles exist and conspiracy content creates self-contained communities, active social information exchange can improve collective intelligence. Results vary depending on whether the study measures platform structure or active social interaction.

How you interact dictates your perspective.

That may be why choosing to engage in structured dialogue with like-minded people might actually clarify your understanding rather than deepening your divide.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A group chat on a Tuesday night.

You scroll through messages from people who agree with you. You share a link and discuss its meaning. You find yourself thinking more clearly about the topic than you did before you opened the app.

The honest summary

The evidence contradicts the proposed angle by showing that social interaction in networks can improve collective intelligence and reduce bias, even if platform algorithms and specific content types contribute to polarization.

The studies differ in their findings because some focus on the structural dynamics of content recommendation (filter bubbles) while others measure the outcomes of active social information exchange.
