Does your commute dictate your mood?

File no. 0117October 18, 2026Modern society

Walking to work may increase leisure time satisfaction.

While walking to work is linked to higher leisure satisfaction and less strain, it is also associated with lower subjective well-being compared to other modes of transport.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Subjective well-being of residents who commute by walking or cycling was significantly lower than that of those who commute by other transportation modes.

    “The subjective well-being of residents who commute by walking or cycling was significantly lower than that of those who commute by other transportation modes.”

    Observational study, 2017. Zhu et al., Transportation.

  2. Walking to work is associated with increased leisure time satisfaction and reduced strain.

    “walking to work is associated with increased leisure time satisfaction and reduced strain.”

    Study, 2019. Clark et al., Transportation.

  3. Longer commute times are more strongly negatively associated with the job satisfaction of women compared to men.

    “longer commute times are more strongly negatively associated with the job satisfaction of women compared to men.”

    Study, 2019. Clark et al., Transportation.

  4. Adverse effects of commuting negatively spill-over to home and job.

    “Adverse effects of commuting negatively spill-over to home and job.”

    Systematic review, 2022. Liu et al., Travel Behaviour and Society.

  5. The longer the commute time, the lower the subjective well-being.

    “The regression results indicated that the longer the commute time, the lower the subjective well-being.”

    Observational study, 2017. Zhu et al., Transportation.

  6. The absence of the commute, via working from home, is associated with increased job satisfaction and leisure time satisfaction.

    “The absence of the commute, via working from home, is associated with increased job satisfaction and leisure time satisfaction.”

    Study, 2019. Clark et al., Transportation.

The commute impacts your home and your job.

Adverse effects of commuting spill over into your home life and your work. Longer commutes are linked to lower subjective well-being and lower job satisfaction, especially for women.

The commute is an invisible drain.

That may be why your day feels heavy. The time spent moving between places is not neutral. It changes how you feel about your work and your rest.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The glow of a dashboard in the dark.

You sit in your car. The engine hums. You are between two lives, neither of which feels fully present.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that longer commutes generally correlate with lower well-being and job satisfaction, but the impact varies significantly by transport mode and gender.

The findings are largely based on observational studies and systematic reviews, meaning they show associations rather than proving that commuting causes lower well-being.
