The dark side of modern life

Can bacteria learn to survive every medicine?

File no. 0159October 24, 2026Science and reality

Bacteria evolve resistance in nearly every lineage.

When exposed to specific antimicrobial peptides, 22 out of 24 lineages of Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas fluorescens independently developed heritable resistance.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence backs this.

What the research found

  1. 22/24 lineages of Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas fluorescens independently evolved heritable mechanisms of resistance to pexiganan when propagated in medium supplemented with this antimicrobial peptide for 600-700 generations.

    “In this selection experiment, 22/24 lineages of Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas fluorescens independently evolved heritable mechanisms of resistance to pexiganan, an analogue of magainin, when propagated in medium supplemented with this antimicrobial peptide for 600-700 generations.”

    Experiment, 2005. Perron et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B Biological Sciences.

  2. Emerging contaminants such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), persistent organic pollutants (POPs), and microplastics also significantly influence the emergence, evolution, and dissemination of bacterial antimicrobial resistance.

    “In addition to antibiotics themselves, emerging contaminants such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), persistent organic pollutants (POPs), and microplastics also significantly influence the emergence, evolution, and dissemination of bacterial antimicrobial resistance.”

    Systematic review, 2026. Yang et al., Global Environment Science.

  3. Antibiotic selection can drive the evolution of duplicated antibiotic resistance genes through MGE transposition.

    “Modeling and experiments show that antibiotic selection can drive the evolution of duplicated antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) through MGE transposition.”

    Experiment, 2024. Maddamsetti et al., Nature Communications.

  4. Duplicated antibiotic resistance genes are highly enriched in bacteria isolated from humans and livestock.

    “Duplicated ARGs are highly enriched in bacteria isolated from humans and livestock.”

    Experiment, 2024. Maddamsetti et al., Nature Communications.

  5. The selective pressures caused by increased use and misuse of antimicrobials in medicine and livestock production have accelerated the overall selection of resistant bacteria.

    “The selective pressures caused by increased use and misuse of antimicrobials in medicine and livestock production have accelerated the overall selection of resistant bacteria.”

    Review, 2021. Liu et al., Journal of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy. This shows a link, not a cause.

Selection drives genetic duplication.

Antibiotic use can cause bacteria to duplicate their resistance genes. These duplicated genes are highly present in bacteria found in humans and livestock.

Resistance is an environmental byproduct.

That may be why microscopic pollutants like microplastics and chemicals influence how bacteria evolve and spread resistance across our shared environment.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The water in your sink.

You turn on the tap and watch the water flow over the basin. It carries microscopic remnants of modern life.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that antibiotic use and environmental factors accelerate the selection and spread of resistance genes, including the evolution of resistance to novel antimicrobial peptides.

While many mechanisms are identified, there is an urgent need to further investigate the specific mechanisms of antimicrobial-induced horizontal transfer.
