Does more money always mean more happiness?

File no. 0075October 12, 2026Money and power

Income and happiness do not always move together.

In some parts of the world, the relationship between wealth and well-being becomes negative. This means more money can lead to less happiness in specific regions.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence goes against the popular idea.

What the research found

  1. In North America/Oceania and Sub-Saharan Africa, the marginal effect of income on well-being even becomes negative.

    “In North America/Oceania and Sub-Saharan Africa, the marginal effect even becomes negative.”

    Study, 2015. Opfinger, Applied Economics Letters.

  2. There is a clear positive link between average levels of subjective well-being and GDP per capita across countries.

    “we establish a clear positive link between average levels of subjective well-being and GDP per capita across countries”

    Study, 2008. Stevenson & Wolfers, National Bureau of Economic Research.

  3. Economic growth is associated with rising happiness over time within countries.

    “examining the relationship between changes in subjective well-being and income over time within countries we find economic growth associated with rising happiness.”

    Study, 2008. Stevenson & Wolfers, National Bureau of Economic Research.

  4. Rich countries are happier than slightly poorer ones, and average well-being in a country does rise as average income rises over time.

    “they assert that average well-being in a country does, in fact, rise as average income rises over time, and that rich countries are happier than slightly poorer ones.”

    Study, 2012. Frank, Emotion.

  5. The positive relationship between income and well-being is not significant in Western Europe and Asia.

    “The relationship is not significant in Western Europe and Asia.”

    Study, 2015. Opfinger, Applied Economics Letters.

  6. Relative income is significantly associated with happiness in China, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan.

    “At the same time, relative income is significantly associated with happiness in all four East Asian nations”

    Observational study, 2016. Yamashita et al., Asian Journal Of Social Psychology.

The link depends on where you live.

While rich countries are generally happier than poorer ones, the connection is not consistent everywhere. In Western Europe and Asia, the positive link is not significant.

Your environment shapes your progress.

That may be why your personal success feels different than the national average. Your happiness might be tied more to local context than to your bank balance.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The honest summary

The evidence largely contradicts the proposed angle by showing that happiness often does rise with income, though this varies significantly by region.

The findings are inconsistent across different geographical regions and vary depending on whether the analysis focuses on absolute or relative income.
