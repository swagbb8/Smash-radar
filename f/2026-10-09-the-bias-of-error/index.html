Why do you forgive people but punish machines?

File no. 0053October 9, 2026Technology

You forgive a human but punish an algorithm.

People use automated advice significantly less than human advice after receiving bad information. The evidence is mixed because the impact depends on how information is presented and on your personality traits.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Showing an algorithm's prediction errors in an aggregated format does not decrease trust in the algorithm or the level of algorithm appreciation.

    “Interestingly, neither the extent of higher trust in algorithmic advisors nor the level of algorithm appreciation decreases when individuals are informed of the algorithm’s prediction errors (i. e. , upon presenting prediction performance in an aggregated format).”

    Experiment, 2022. You et al., Journal of Management Information Systems.

  2. After receiving bad advice, people use automated advice significantly less than they use advice from humans.

    “However, after receiving bad advice, utilization of automated advice decreased significantly more than advice from humans.”

    Experiment, 2017. Prahl & Swol, Journal of Forecasting.

  3. Providing a more detailed format for an algorithm's prediction performance reduces algorithm appreciation.

    “By contrast, algorithm appreciation declines when the transparency of the advice source’s prediction performance further increases through an elaborated format.”

    Experiment, 2022. You et al., Journal of Management Information Systems.

  4. Increasing a task’s perceived objectivity increases trust in and use of algorithms for that task.

    “However, they show that perceived task objectivity is malleable and that increasing a task’s perceived objectivity increases trust in and use of algorithms for that task.”

    Study, 2019. Castelo et al., Springer eBooks.

  5. People describe themselves as having much more in common with human advisors than automated advisors even when there is no interpersonal relationship.

    “We also find that decision makers describe themselves as having much more in common with human than automated advisors despite there being no interpersonal relationship in our study.”

    Experiment, 2017. Prahl & Swol, Journal of Forecasting.

  6. Algorithm appreciation is reduced for individuals who have a lower dispositional need for cognition.

    “Finally, we identify a boundary condition: algorithm appreciation is reduced for individuals with a lower dispositional need for cognition.”

    Experiment, 2022. You et al., Journal of Management Information Systems.

Format dictates your trust.

Showing algorithm errors in an aggregated format does not decrease your appreciation for the system. However, providing a more detailed format for those same errors does reduce your appreciation.

The bias of the interface.

That may be why you feel a deeper connection to human advisors even without a personal relationship. Your brain may be wired to prioritize human flaws over technical ones.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A navigation error.

You are driving through a city at night. The gps tells you to turn into a dead end. You feel a flash of irritation toward the screen that you would not feel toward a human passenger.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that while humans are more forgiving of errors than algorithms, the impact of showing those errors depends heavily on how the information is presented and the nature of the task.

The research highlights that the effect of algorithm aversion is highly dependent on variables like transparency format, task objectivity, and individual personality traits.
