Your brain is being hijacked

Are you choosing your interests, or are they being curated for you?

File no. 0152October 23, 2026Modern society

Algorithms structure what you see but may not change what you believe.

Evidence shows recommender systems limit viewpoint diversity and structure information exposure. However, the evidence for direct ideological polarization is limited and may be driven by human cognitive mechanisms.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Echo chambers can emerge as a consequence of cognitive mechanisms, such as confirmation bias, even without any social or technological filters.

    “Simulations show that, even without any social or technological filters, echo chambers emerge as a consequence of cognitive mechanisms, such as confirmation bias”

    Study, 2018. Geschke et al., British Journal of Social Psychology.

  2. Direct evidence of recursive feedback was limited to a subset of studies, while others examined component processes or addressed feedback conceptually.

    “Direct evidence of recursive feedback was limited to a subset of studies, while others examined component processes or addressed feedback conceptually.”

    Systematic review, 2026. C P & Shukla, Frontiers in sociology.

  3. Evidence for recommender-mediated structuring of informational exposure is more extensive and direct than evidence for attitudinal or ideological polarization.

    “Evidence was more extensive and direct for recommender-mediated structuring and differentiation of informational exposure than for attitudinal or ideological polarization.”

    Systematic review, 2026. C P & Shukla, Frontiers in sociology.

  4. Algorithmic systems structurally amplify ideological homogeneity and limit viewpoint diversity.

    “algorithmic systems structurally amplify ideological homogeneity, reinforcing selective exposure and limiting viewpoint diversity”

    Systematic review, 2025. Ahmmad et al., Societies.

  5. Echo chambers and filter bubbles exist in recommender systems and raise concerns for diversity and fairness.

    “Our review confirms that echo chambers and filter bubbles do exist in recommender systems and that they raise concerns for diversity and fairness.”

    Systematic review, 2025. Saissi et al., International Journal of Advanced Computer Science and Applications.

The architecture of your feed narrows your options.

Algorithmic systems amplify ideological homogeneity and create filter bubbles. These systems structure your informational exposure, creating environments with less diversity and raising concerns for fairness.

Your preferences are shaped by a loop.

That may be why your feed feels familiar. While the technology limits the variety of information you see, your own confirmation bias may be what deepens your existing views.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

The scroll at 11 p.m.

You sit in a dark room. You swipe through a feed of content that looks exactly like what you saw an hour ago. Every video feels like a natural progression.

The honest summary

The sources confirm that algorithms create environments with less diversity, but they also show that these effects are complex, potentially influenced by human psychology rather than just the technology.

The evidence base suffers from geographic bias toward Western contexts, limited longitudinal research, and methodological fragmentation.
