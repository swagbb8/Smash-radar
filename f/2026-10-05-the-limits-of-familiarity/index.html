The world you don’t see

Do you like things because you choose them?

File no. 0006October 5, 2026Modern society

Repeated exposure does not always increase liking.

While visual familiarity can shape preference, the evidence is mixed. The effect is often small and lacks long-term proof.

Established. Several studies combined, or official data. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Repeated exposure to vegetables has small effect sizes and limited evidence suggests long-term benefits.

    “Effect sizes, however, are small, and limited evidence suggests long-term benefits.”

    Meta-analysis, 2018. Appleton et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.

  2. Repeated exposure to visual stimuli is linked to an inverted-U shaped curve of liking, but this effect was not found for auditory stimuli.

    “the mere exposure effect was characterized by a positive slope and negative quadratic effect consistent with an inverted-U shaped curve. In fact, such curves were associated with (a) all visual, but not auditory stimuli”

    Meta-analysis, 2017. Montoya et al., Psychological Bulletin.

  3. The act of responding to measurements can make subjects' perceptions or behaviors on a topic change for the better.

    “This systematic review found significant potential for the mere-measurement effect to shape respondents' behaviors or perceptions for the better”

    Meta-analysis, 2025. Long et al., Quality of Life Research.

  4. Repeated exposure to one and a variety of vegetables, along with the use of rewards, is recommended for increasing vegetable liking and consumption.

    “we recommend the use of repeated exposure to one and a variety of vegetables, and the use of rewards, for increasing vegetable liking and consumption.”

    Meta-analysis, 2018. Appleton et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.

  5. Using a sticker reward with a repeated exposure strategy makes children's liking of a vegetable increase at post-intervention.

    “Both EO and E + R increased liking at post-intervention compared with control and no further change occurred over the follow-up period.”

    Randomized trial, 2011. Corsini et al., Public Health Nutrition.

The limits of familiarity.

The impact of repeated exposure depends on the stimulus. It follows an inverted-U shape for visuals but was not found for auditory stimuli.

The mechanism of change.

That may be why you feel your tastes shifting. Responding to measurements can alter your perceptions. Rewards and specific strategies may also influence your behavior.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A dinner table.

You sit in a chair. You look at a vegetable on your plate for the third time this week. You reach for it.

The honest summary

The evidence shows that repeated exposure can increase liking, but its effectiveness depends on the type of stimulus, the use of rewards, and the duration of the exposure.

Studies on repeated exposure are limited by study design, compliance, and reporting, with some showing only small effect sizes and limited long-term benefits.
