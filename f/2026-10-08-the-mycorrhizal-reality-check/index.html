Do trees really talk through fungi?

File no. 0034October 8, 2026Science and reality

Trees may share resources but their importance is unclear.

While common mycorrhizal networks can move zinc and water, their overall significance to the forest remains uncertain. The evidence is mixed.

Supported. At least one solid peer-reviewed study. The evidence is mixed.

What the research found

  1. Common mycorrhizal networks may have negative effects on plant performance.

    “We also emphasize possible negative effects of common mycorrhizal networks on plant performance and question popular views that mycorrhizal networks may offer a panacea for enhancing ecosystem services.”

    Study, 2021. Alaux et al., Plants People Planet.

  2. Only a small percentage of mycorrhizal roots and extraradical hyphae were involved with water transfer among plants.

    “The observed patterns of dye distribution also indicated that only a small percentage of mycorrhizal roots and extraradical hyphae were involved with water transfer among plants.”

    Experiment, 2007. Egerton‐Warburton et al., Journal of Experimental Botany.

  3. The importance of common mycorrhizal networks remains unclear.

    “It concluded that while there is evidence for C movement among plants, the importance of CMNs remains unclear, as noted by others too (Henriksson et al . 2023).”

    Study, 2023. Klein et al., Open Research Europe.

  4. Direct transfer of zinc from donor leaves to receiver shoots was demonstrated mediated by CMN.

    “A direct transfer of Zn was demonstrated from donor leaves to receiver shoots mediated by CMN.”

    Study, 2021. Cardini et al., Environmental Microbiology.

  5. Seedlings that were not by CMNs not et al. CMN effects on seedling and growth in Douglas-fir forests have shown greater effects on where they had to mycorrhizal root and soil.

    “CMN effects on seedling and growth in Douglas-fir forests have shown greater effects on where they had to mycorrhizal root and soil”

    Experiment, 2025. Simard et al., Frontiers in Forests and Global Change.

The network is more complex than a simple lifeline.

Research shows these fungal networks can move zinc from donor leaves to receiver shoots. However, only a small percentage of roots and hyphae are involved in water transfer.

Nature is not a perfect cooperation.

These networks might not always help. They can have negative effects on plant performance, suggesting that the underground connection is not a guaranteed benefit for every tree.

This part is our reading of the evidence, not a finding.

A quiet forest floor in the morning.

You walk past a cluster of Douglas-fir trees. Beneath your feet, the soil contains the roots and fungi that determine which seedlings survive.

The honest summary

The evidence confirms that resource transfer occurs through these networks but highlights significant uncertainty regarding their ecological significance and potential drawbacks.

The research notes a need for greater insight into the ubiquity, functioning, and response to management interventions of these networks.
